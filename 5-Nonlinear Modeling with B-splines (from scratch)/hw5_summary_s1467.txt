The objective of this project was to model daily household electricity consumption and predict high-demand operational days using historical weather and calendar features. Initial exploratory data analysis revealed a severe non-linear, U-shaped relationship between temperature and electricity demand, reflecting heavy power usage during extreme hot and cold weather. Conversely, secondary features like humidity and weekend status demonstrated simple additive effects. Notably, we discovered a near-zero linear correlation between temperature and humidity. This independence was highly beneficial, as it prevented linear multicollinearity and allowed us to confidently isolate their respective impacts on the power grid.

Because a standard linear model could not capture the temperature extremes, we engineered a highly flexible non-linear architecture using cubic B-splines. We implemented the basis functions entirely from scratch utilizing the recursive Cox-de Boor algorithm. To determine the optimal flexibility of the curve without overfitting, we utilized a custom five-fold cross-validation pipeline, which identified three internal knots as the ideal parameter. Furthermore, we strictly clamped the boundary knots at zero and forty degrees Celsius to ensure the mathematical stability of the curves when scoring unseen test data with potentially unprecedented weather conditions.

With the design matrix constructed, we solved the continuous consumption regression task using a custom Ordinary Least Squares implementation based on matrix algebra, and tackled the high-demand classification task using logistic regression. A critical statistical requirement for both models was the deliberate exclusion of a global intercept. Because B-spline basis functions natively form a partition of unity, summing to exactly one at any given point, leaving an intercept in the matrix would have triggered perfect multicollinearity and matrix singularity. We also prioritized parsimony by treating humidity and weekends as linear baseline shifts, avoiding complex interaction terms to preserve out-of-sample generalization. Ultimately, these robust mathematical controls resulted in a stable, highly accurate predictive framework.
